athletIQ

Founder. Rebuilt from scratch in November 2025; 314 commits since. Flask, SQLite, Selenium, SciPy, Railway.

College track recruiting still runs on spreadsheets and a single personal best. athletIQ is what a coaching staff uses instead: it finds recruits across every state, puts every event on one scale, forecasts where an athlete is headed, and tracks them from first contact to the roster.

  1. Scraper service. Fetching runs as its own process that the app calls over an authenticated API, so one slow page never stalls a coach's session.
  2. Every state, every season. Ranking pages are keyed by season IDs; the app discovers them for all 50 states and DC and caches them to disk.
  3. Background jobs. A national search can touch thousands of athletes, so it runs off the request thread while the page polls for progress.
  4. Tools on top. Plain-English search that fills in the filters, a recruit pipeline from first contact to commitment, and roster tracking with eligibility and redshirts.

Search a whole country against your standards

A coach sets a target mark for each event, a tolerance band, states and a graduating class. A background job pages through every matching state ranking, scores each athlete's distance from the standard (within 1% scores 100, falling off past 5%), merges each athlete's events across states by profile, and rewards athletes who fit in more than one event. Results export straight to a mail-merge CSV with school contacts.

One scale for every event

An 800m runner and a 5000m runner can't be compared on time. athletIQ converts every race to VDOT, Jack Daniels' oxygen-cost model, and uses it to compare recruits across events, project high school marks to college, and check how a recruit fits the current roster.

VO₂ = −4.60 + 0.182258v + 0.000104v², divided by the fraction of max sustainable for that duration. Going back from VDOT to a time is a bisection search.

Forecasting from training, not just results

The forecaster fits a Banister fitness-fatigue model to each athlete: every training load adds fitness that fades slowly and fatigue that fades fast, and performance is the difference. athletIQ fits the gains and time constants to that athlete's own races with regularized least squares, then samples 4,000 parameter sets from the fit's covariance to draw an honest range.

Fitness-fatigue modelDrag the weekly mileage bars. The band is 300 sampled athletes.

Here τ-fitness and τ-fatigue sit at the product's priors (45 and 11 days) and the band varies them with a ±15% responder spread; the product fits them per athlete.

Benchmark before you ship

To decide how athletIQ should flag injury risk, I tested three approaches on a public dataset of 42,798 athlete-weeks from 74 runners, splitting folds by athlete so no runner appeared in both training and testing. Plain logistic regression beat XGBoost and the acute-to-chronic workload ratio that coaches commonly use.

AUC is how well a model ranks injured weeks above healthy ones; 0.5 is chance. The honest result is modest, which is the point of measuring.

Pan-Out, the same engine for other sports

An athletIQ product that estimates the odds a junior athlete reaches each level in tennis, chess, baseball and hockey, because ratings ignore age: a 13-year-old and a 17-year-old with the same rating are not the same prospect. Five gradient-boosted models each answer one question (trajectory, dominance, clutch, load, performance against same-age peers), logistic heads stack them per level, and isotonic regression calibrates the odds. Players are split by birth year into train, stack, calibrate and test cohorts.

On simulated histories it ranks future pros far better than rating alone (AUC 0.94 against 0.84 in tennis). Backtested on real ATP careers, that edge nearly vanished, 0.844 against 0.841, though it still found about 2.6 times more future pros among players the rankings rated low. The gap measures how much the simulator flattered the model, and it's the most useful thing the project taught me.

SECRET, a 48-volt race car

Founder and co-head, Sidwell Electric Car Race Engineering Team. 2023 to now. Raised $7K; working alongside UMD's Formula SAE team.

The Electric Vehicle Grand Prix is an endurance race: whoever covers the most distance in one hour on a fixed battery wins. Drive too slow and you finish with charge left over; too fast and the pack dies before the flag. We built our car from the competition kit, then rebuilt it: an aluminum chassis, a new battery pack, 3D-printed wheel covers, and an Arduino system that tells the driver how hard to push.

Thick lines carry the 48V traction current on 6-gauge cable; thin lines are low-voltage signals. Select any part.

Pacing to an energy budget

Every lap, the Arduino reads the amp-hours used from the Cycle Analyst, spreads what's left of the battery evenly over the laps left in the hour, and tells the driver on the dash display to speed up or back off.

It matters because energy per lap climbs steeply with speed. Aerodynamic drag grows with the square of speed, so the power to beat it grows with the cube, and lead-acid batteries also give up usable capacity at high current (the Peukert effect). Going out too fast is punished twice.

Modeled car, not measured from ours: 150 kg with driver, drag area 0.30 m², rolling coefficient 0.015, 80% drivetrain efficiency, 48V 26Ah pack, Peukert exponent 1.15, 0.6-mile lap.

Raced the DC Electric Vehicle Grand Prix in 2024, 2025 and 2026, won its top 3D printing prize, and was invited to the 2027 Dubai Grand Prix.

Meridian

Lead researcher, UVA School of Data Science, with Prof. Adam Tashman. 2025 to now. LightGBM, sentence-transformers, FastAPI.

Venture capital is a market for founders, and it has frictions: who you are and where you're from shift who funds you. Meridian measures those frictions and turns the model around into a tool that matches underserved founders with investors likely to fund them.

It is a learning-to-rank problem, not a classifier. For each startup, a LightGBM LambdaRank model orders 48,046 investors, trained on about 907,000 startup-investor pairs. Each pair gets 16 features, including embedding similarity between the startup's description and the investor's portfolio (MiniLM sentence embeddings), stage and check-size fit, geography, and how concentrated the investor's past deals are. The split is by startup, so no company appears in both training and test, and the candidate pool only includes investors active in that deal's year.

We deployed it with founder organizations including 434, BEA, StartOut, Astia and BGV, serving investor matches to more than 20,000 underserved founders. The paper is in review; I presented it at the 2026 UVA Undergraduate Research Symposium and ICMBF 2026.

ReachBot

Summer associate, Reach Capital, 2026. Python, Gemini, NumPy, Streamlit, GitHub Actions.

Reach Capital's portfolio founders get a library of hundreds of guides and decks and 137 recorded expert sessions, but as guests they can't search it with AI. ReachBot lets them ask in plain English and answers with citations to the exact page or recording.

It's retrieval-augmented generation, not a fine-tuned model. A weekly GitHub Actions job exports the Notion library and Zoom transcripts to markdown with their source links, splits 696 sources into 11,244 overlapping 400-word chunks, and embeds each as a 3,072-dimension vector. At question time, a small model first rewrites follow-ups like "how do I redeem it?" into standalone queries; cosine similarity then runs as a brute-force NumPy scan in milliseconds, since a vector database buys nothing at this size; and Gemini 2.5 Flash answers only from the retrieved passages, citing each one.

Why not paste everything into a million-token context window? The library is about 6 million tokens, and even if it fit, that would cost about $1.80 a question against roughly half a cent with retrieval. The weekly refresh costs about a cent.

The retrieval gateHow much ReachBot says depends on how strong the evidence is.

The 0.70 and 0.60 cutoffs come from measured scores: on-topic questions land near 0.75, adjacent near 0.66, off-topic near 0.54.

Reading a script no one can read

Independent research with Louie Merriam, 2023 to now. Published in the International Journal of Computer Applications, Vol. 187, No. 64. Funded by an Emergent Ventures grant.

The Indus script is around 4,500 years old, has hundreds of signs, and has no bilingual key. It is probably logo-syllabic: some signs stand for whole words, others for sounds. Our paper separates the two using only where signs appear.

    An 18-inscription toy corpus, so the method fits on screen. The paper runs on the full corpus and tests each association statistically.

    Can hardware prove a paper is real?

    GMU Aspiring Scientists Summer Internship Program, with Prof. Jiasun Li, Costello College of Business. 2025. Abstract published in the Journal of Student-Scientists' Research.

    Journals now require replication packages, but nothing proves the code was actually run as claimed, on the stated inputs, to produce the published tables. Intel TDX runs code inside a hardware-isolated confidential VM whose memory the host can't read, and it can attest cryptographically to exactly what ran.

    1. Pull the package. A 2024 Management Science paper's code and data.
    2. Stand up a TDX VM. A confidential VM on Azure and again on Google Cloud.
    3. Rebuild the stack. Install the paper's software (Stata, R and others) inside the VM.
    4. Run and compare. Check outputs against the paper; log runtime, cost and every failure to a shared database.

    19 of 27 replications succeeded across both clouds. Most failures traced to code missing from the package; others to software we couldn't license on a free-credit budget, like SAS. Runs averaged about 2 hours 10 minutes and $1.33. I was one of 15 interns on the project and presented it at the program's symposium.
